Image-restoration comparisons often omit the arm that does nothing. We report
three evidence-bounded comparisons from one project in which that arm was
retained. At toy scale, two matched-budget networks both finish below the
degraded input: \ToyConv{} and \ToyTrans{} dB versus \ToyDegraded{} dB. Giving
the better arm a \BudgetFold{} budget lowers its training loss by
\ToyLossDrop{} times but moves fidelity \ToyLongRegression{} dB in the wrong
direction, so the toy run cannot rank a restoration method above its input.

At subset scale, a released diffusion model completes on \SubsetN{} crops and
loses to bicubic on \LossesBicubic{}; the mean fidelity gap is
\GapBicubicAbs{} dB, \NoiseMargin{} times the largest shift the same model
shows between two runs on the same crops. No perceptual measure was computed,
so this is a fidelity-only ranking rather than a verdict on overall image
quality. The earlier full-set census contains \CensusTotal{} inputs:
\CensusSkipped{} are skipped by the memory rule and only \CensusRan{} survive;
the survivors lack reference images and are not scored, making the measured
set a property of hardware-dependent selection.

The practical recommendation is narrow: print the trivial arm beside the
method and describe any win by that arm as a statement about the measured
endpoint. This audit does not promote a perceptual, mechanism, or
population-wide conclusion.
